How the BYK Snowpack Model works

Banff, Yoho and Kootenay · Parks Canada Visitor Safety

These are unverified model simulations. Nothing on this site has been checked by a person. Use it as one line of evidence next to field observations, the station network and professional judgement — never on its own.

1. Weather inputs

  • Actuals — hourly air temperature, relative humidity, wind, snow height and precipitation from the Parks Canada FTS stations in and around BYK, read from the Rockies Weather Data Explorer (/api/fts).
  • Forecasts — ECCC HRDPS (2.5 km) for the first 48 h and RDPS (10 km) beyond, requested through the Parks Wx Fx cached Open-Meteo proxy (/api/forecast) at about 70 model columns: every station, every named forecast point and a coarse fill grid. Open-Meteo is called directly only if the proxy is unavailable.

2. Getting weather to every cell

The model grid is about 5 km (0.045° × 0.07°). Each cell is simulated at its mean terrain elevation (the mean of every pixel of the open Terrarium elevation tiles inside the cell, about 190 m apart). For every hour:

  1. Model first guess. Inverse-distance weighting of the six nearest model columns. Temperature is moved to the cell elevation with the lapse rate fitted across all columns that hour, so inversions are kept. Precipitation is scaled +5 % per 100 m.
  2. Temperature profile. Each hour the station temperature residuals are fitted against station elevation (1310–2930 m), so the model's vertical profile is corrected everywhere, including ridge-top inversions the model misses. The fit is shrunk toward no correction when few stations report.
  3. Station correction. At each station the difference between the observation and the first guess is a residual (temperature, humidity; for temperature, what the elevation fit leaves) or a log-ratio (wind, 24 h precipitation). Residuals are spread with a Gaussian kernel of 20–40 km horizontally and 300–1200 m vertically, and shrink to zero away from stations. Ridge stations therefore correct alpine cells and valley stations correct valley cells. Wind corrections use only representative anemometers.
  4. Ridge-top wind. The model's 10 m wind is an average over a 2.5 km grid cell and reads 2–4 times lower than the ridge anemometers (Vulture Peak, Bosworth Upper, Whymper, Lookout, Simpson Upper). A separate ridge wind scales the model's mean wind over the surrounding ~40 km by each ridge station's ratio of observed to model wind, spread horizontally over ~15 km and pulled toward the average of the ridge stations elsewhere. Its direction is the model's flow over the same ~40 km, which matches the ridge anemometers better than the 10 m direction of the nearest grid cells. Through the forecast each station's ratio fades into its learned average, like the other corrections.
  5. Precipitation from stations. At the snow-height stations (Bow Summit, Bosworth Lower, Stanley Lower, Simpson Lower, Sunshine, Pika Run, Coleman, Big Bend) a flat snow column is run with the station's weather, and snow falls on it only when the measured snow height rises above the simulated one, as SNOWPACK does at automatic stations. That snowfall already allows for the settling snowpack underneath. Snow-height readings are quality-checked first: jumps faster than snow can fall, and jumps that don't hold for the next hours, are dropped. The weighing gauges (Bow Summit, Skoki) are used with a snow undercatch allowance of 15–30 %. Station precipitation is compared with the model's over the 72 h ending each 17:00, and the ratio corrects the last 24 h within about 40 km and 1200 m of elevation. The station's own point takes its snow-height snowfall directly.
  6. Forecast hours. The last 6 hours of temperature, humidity and wind residuals fade (6 h, 6 h and 12 h e-folding) into a bias learned for each station and hour of day over roughly the last two weeks of analysis, which then persists through the forecast. Forecast precipitation is the model amount scaled by each station's recent precipitation ratio.
  7. Rain and snow are split on air temperature (all snow ≤ 0 °C, all rain ≥ +2 °C). Incoming longwave is estimated from temperature, humidity and model cloud cover.

3. The snow model

Every cell and point is simulated as five snow columns: flat ground and 38° slopes facing N, E, S and W. Map values are averages over those five. The model is a compact multilayer model written for this site in the style of SNOWPACK and Crocus. It is not SLF's SNOWPACK program.

ProcessTreatment
New snowDensity, dendricity and sphericity from air temperature and wind (Crocus, Vionnet et al. 2012).
WindSnow on the slopes is moved by the ridge-top wind in the alpine (2400 m and up), by a wind between the local and ridge-top wind at treeline, and by the local wind below 1800 m. Snowfall loads lee slopes and unloads windward slopes by up to ±35 %. Loose snow drifts from windward to lee above a threshold wind, and is deposited as dense wind-affected layers. The flat field is a sheltered study plot and sees only the local wind, and the slopes' energy balance also uses the local wind.
Energy balanceSlope shortwave (sun geometry, no terrain shading) with an albedo that falls with the snow's age and with the hours it has spent wet; longwave with sky view; stability-corrected sensible and latent heat; rain heat. Solved implicitly, with heat conduction through the layers (Calonne et al. 2011 conductivity) to 0 °C ground.
WaterMelt, refreeze and bucket percolation (3 % irreducible water). Refrozen wet layers become melt-freeze crusts.
SettlementCrocus overburden viscosity plus Anderson (1976) destructive metamorphism of new snow.
MetamorphismDendricity, sphericity and grain size change with temperature, temperature gradient and liquid water: rounding below about 5 K/m and faceting above about 7 K/m, plus wet grain growth. Large faceted grains round slowly, and a layer that has faceted stays FCxr, or DH once it has grown depth hoar, rather than turning into rounded grains.
Surface hoarGrows from deposition on calm (< 3.5 m/s), clear nights. Wind, sun or melt destroys it. When buried it is kept as its own layer if the crystals are 2 mm or larger.

Grain types are mapped from these state variables to AVID's five groups: PP/DF, RG/FCxr, SH/DH/FC, MFcr and MF. Hand hardness comes from density and grain type, after the regressions of Geldsetzer & Jamieson (2000).

4. Avalanche-problem indices

Map propertyDefinition
Persistent weak layersThreshold-sum ("lemons") structural instability of each buried weak zone: adjacent SH, DH, FC or FCxr layers are scored together, on their largest grains and softest hardness against the slab above and the layer below (Schweizer & Jamieson 2007): persistent grain type, grain size ≥ 1.25 mm, hardness ≤ F+, grain-size difference ≥ 0.75 mm, hardness difference ≥ 1.7 steps, and depth 18–94 cm. The index is a logistic curve centred between 4 and 5 lemons, reduced when the slab is thin or very deep. A layer counts as a critical PWL when the index is ≥ 70 % at 18–150 cm depth. Weak zones deeper than 94 cm are scored again as if they had the depth lemon and reported in the text as a deep persistent layer.
New snowFrom the settled thickness of snow deposited in the last 24 h (centred at 20 cm) and 72 h (centred at 30 cm).
Wind slabFrom the thickness of dry, wind-affected layers deposited in the last 96 h in the top metre (centred at 15 cm).
Wet snowFrom the mean liquid water content of the top 50 cm relative to 3 % (the SNOWPACK LWC index), and from rain in the last 24 h.
Hazard1 − (1 − new)(1 − wind)(1 − 0.6 · PWL)(1 − wet). This is an index, not a danger rating.
Ski penSNOWPACK's empirical 0.8 × 43.3 / ρ̄, where ρ̄ is the mean density of the top 30 cm.

5. Runs and dates

The model runs every hour at :25, after the station archive syncs. Each run advances the stored snowpacks through the newly observed hours; this is the analysis. It then re-runs copies through the forecast about every 3 hours. Each date on the map is the state at 17:00 Mountain time. Past dates are analysis; today (before 17:00), tomorrow and +2 days are forecast. The season starts on 1 September.

6. Past seasons

Choose a past season (2023–24, 2024–25, 2025–26) in the season picker to browse it the way it was simulated: the same model and grid, run through the winter as a reanalysis. The weather comes from archived HRDPS runs (the Open-Meteo Historical Forecast API), with RDPS filling any gaps. It is corrected with the station actuals in the Rockies Weather Data Explorer archive, which for these seasons hold air temperature, humidity, wind, snow height and gauge precipitation from early October 2023 (some stations start later). There is no forecast step, so every date is analysis.

7. Known limitations

  • A 5 km cell is represented by one elevation, so terrain within the cell is smoothed out. Named points are also run at fixed ALP, TL and BTL elevations.
  • There is no terrain shading and no glacier ice. Snow height is assimilated only as snowfall at the stations; melt and settlement are not corrected.
  • The problem indices are heuristics tuned to behave sensibly. They are not statistically calibrated to avalanche observations the way AVID's random-forest instability model is.
  • Model quality depends on station uptime. Open Run & station corrections on the map to see which stations fed the latest run and how large the corrections were.
